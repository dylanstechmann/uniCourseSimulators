# Magnetic vectors, fields and force-work distinctions

## Learning objectives

1. Calculate signed magnetic-force components using a stated charge and coordinate system.
2. Relate ideal circular motion and selected current-source fields to their model assumptions.
3. Distinguish magnetic deflection, electrical work and incomplete force or field evidence.

## The cross product contains the direction

For a nonrelativistic point charge in prescribed fields, the Lorentz force is q(E+v×B). The magnetic part is perpendicular to velocity, so its instantaneous power on that point charge is q(v×B)·v=0. This statement concerns the magnetic force contribution, not every force or energy transfer in an electromagnetic system. Electric fields can still do work.

Choose right-handed axes x,y,z. Stipulate a synthetic positive charge q=2.0 nC, velocity v=(3.0,0,0) m/s and magnetic field B=(0,0,0.40) T. Because x crossed with z points in negative y, magnetic force is (0,−2.4,0) nN. Adding magnitudes alone would lose the sign that determines the initial deflection.

Reversing q reverses force for the same v and B. Reversing B or v also reverses the magnetic cross product, while reversing both leaves it unchanged. A positive-charge right-hand construction must therefore be reversed for negative charge. The geometry is defined by the stated axes rather than by an unlabeled page arrow or a presumed observer orientation.

## A restricted circular orbit

For a separate uniform-field motion with no electric force, stipulate mass m=1.0×10⁻¹² kg for the same positive q and perpendicular initial speed 3.0 m/s. The speed stays fixed while direction changes. Equating magnetic-force magnitude to mv²/r gives radius r=mv/(|q|B)=0.00375 m, or 3.75 mm. The angular frequency magnitude is |q|B/m=800 rad/s.

The constructed mass and charge do not identify a real ion, aerosol or laboratory sample. These numbers illustrate a mathematical point-particle model, with no apparatus trajectory or confinement procedure. At an initial velocity partly parallel to B, only the perpendicular component sets the circular radius; the parallel component remains unchanged under the stated magnetic-only force, producing a helical path.

Constant speed does not mean zero acceleration. A circular path has acceleration toward its instantaneous center even though kinetic energy is unchanged. This is the same vector-versus-speed distinction developed in mechanics. A trajectory changing speed under the declared magnetic-only model would signal another force or an inconsistent calculation.

## Forces on ideal current elements

For a straight wire segment in a uniform field, the selected ideal relation is F=I l×B, where l points along conventional current and has the segment's length. With I=0.20 A, l=(0.10,0,0) m and B=(0,0,0.40) T, force is negative y with magnitude 0.0080 N, or 8.0 mN. A segment parallel to B gives zero magnetic force in this model.

A real closed circuit includes return paths and supports. A force on one chosen segment is not automatically the net force on the whole loop. In a uniform field, forces on opposite segments of a symmetric loop can cancel in translation while still producing a torque. The current supply, mechanical constraints and all relevant segments belong in a complete energy or force account.

The fact that magnetic point-charge force does no work does not forbid a current-carrying apparatus from moving or exchanging mechanical energy. Charge constraints, electric driving and the rest of the circuit matter. Applying the point-charge dot-product result directly as “no electromagnetic machine can do work” would change the system boundary without justification.

## Two current-source field models

For an ideal infinitely long straight wire, the field magnitude at radius r is μ₀I/(2πr). Stipulate μ₀=4π×10⁻⁷ H/m for these teaching calculations. At I=0.20 A and r=0.010 m, magnitude is 4.0 µT. Its direction circles the wire according to conventional-current orientation. The expression neglects finite ends and the actual return path.

For a separate ideal long solenoid in vacuum with turns per length n=1000 m⁻¹ and current 0.20 A, the interior field approximation is μ₀nI≈251.327 µT. The approximation assumes a region well away from ends and the selected uniform winding model. A ferromagnetic core, finite geometry or saturation would require different information and cannot be added by silently changing μ₀.

Ampere's law gives a circulation constraint in a suitable steady-current setting. Just as Gauss's law needs symmetry to determine a local field simply, a known enclosed current does not by itself make B uniform everywhere on an arbitrarily chosen path. Nearby conductors, return currents and time-dependent fields can alter the local pattern while preserving an appropriate complete law.

## Worked example

Write v and B components first, evaluate v×B and then multiply by the signed charge for −2.4 nN in y. For the independent magnetic-only circular model, use force balance for radius 3.75 mm and ω=800 rad/s, checking unchanged kinetic energy. For the wire segment, use the current-direction length vector. Compare the ideal long-wire and solenoid field calculations only within their different geometrical assumptions.

## Common mistakes

Do not use a positive-charge direction for an electron without reversing it, treat speed as a vector component or conclude that a single segment's force is the whole circuit force. A circulation law does not establish a local magnitude without sufficient symmetry. Magnetic deflection and electric work require separate accounting.

## Limits of this lesson

All charges, masses, currents and geometries are synthetic. Uniform prescribed fields, point particles, infinite wires and long solenoids are restricted teaching models. They establish no actual exposure threshold, tissue effect or equipment setting. No procedure is supplied. Original instruction has substantial AI assistance; human review and workload measurement remain absent. The course stays partial, unreviewed and formative-only.
