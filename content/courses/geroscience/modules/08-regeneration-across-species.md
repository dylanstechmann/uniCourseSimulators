# Regeneration across species: what regrows, what scars, and what counts as evidence

Some animals regrow limbs, hearts and whole bodies, and humans regrow a liver and heal a skin wound with a scar. Understanding what separates these outcomes is the scientific core of regenerative medicine, and it is also a place where loose language is common: "proliferating cells near the wound", "healing", and "regeneration" are different claims with different evidence. This lesson sets out the main model systems, the measurements used, and what a regeneration claim needs.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Calculate and compare scar fraction between injury conditions from replicate measurements.
2. Distinguish proliferation markers, hypertrophy and true regeneration, and name the evidence needed to claim regeneration.
3. Explain what comparisons across species and across developmental time can and cannot say about adult mammals.

## Models that regenerate

**Planarian flatworms** regenerate whole bodies from fragments. Their adult stem cells, called neoblasts, are widespread and can give rise to all cell types, and regeneration of missing parts depends on them.

**Salamanders** regrow limbs through a **blastema**, a mass of progenitor cells that forms at the wound. Many cells there arise by dedifferentiation of local tissue, and the blastema reforms the missing structure with the correct pattern.

**Zebrafish** regenerate fins, spinal cord and the heart. After surgical removal of about a fifth of the ventricle, the heart in a 2002 study regenerated the missing muscle over weeks with little scarring, and cardiomyocytes were the source of new muscle.

**Neonatal mice** can regenerate the heart for a short time. In a 2011 study, resection of the ventricular apex at one day after birth was followed by regrowth with minimal scarring, whereas the same injury at seven days after birth left a scar and did not regenerate. The window closes within about a week.

## Mammals in adulthood

Adult mammals regenerate some tissues: the liver restores its mass mostly by existing hepatocytes dividing, the blood and gut epithelium are renewed continuously by stem cells, and the mouse digit tip regrows. The heart and the central nervous system mostly respond to injury with scar and limited renewal. Adult human cardiomyocytes renew slowly, at low rates measured by isotope methods, so heart attacks leave scars that do not turn back into muscle.

These contrasts give the research questions. Why does the same organ regenerate in a fish and in a day-old mouse and not in an adult mouse? Candidates include changes in cell-cycle capacity, polyploidy of cardiomyocytes, immune and inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix, thyroid hormone and metabolic state. None is settled as a full explanation, and a difference between species is not by itself a mechanism.

## What counts as evidence of regeneration

An increase in dividing cells near an injury is not regeneration. The tissue has to be restored. A fair claim needs several kinds of evidence together.

1. **Restored structure and mass.** The lost tissue is replaced, measured by histology or imaging, not only a thicker wall from larger cells.
2. **Restored function.** For the heart, for example, pumping function recovers.
3. **Cell origin.** Lineage tracing, which permanently labels a defined cell population and its descendants, shows which cells formed the new tissue. Without it, proliferation near the wound could come from other cell types.
4. **Appropriate controls.** A sham operation controls for surgery, and uninjured tissue sets baseline.
5. **Time course.** Regeneration is a process; one time point can capture a transient state.
6. **Scar assessment.** A scar is the alternative outcome, so its extent is the usual contrast.

**Hypertrophy**, enlargement of existing cells, can restore mass and wall thickness without new cells and is a common compensatory response. A proliferation label such as EdU incorporation shows DNA synthesis; in some cardiomyocytes DNA synthesis occurs without division (polyploidy), so DNA synthesis alone does not prove new cells.

## Worked example

The mean scar area is 3.2% after day-1 resection and 18.4% after day-7 resection, a difference of 15.2 percentage points. With a sham mean of 0.5%, the day-1 value is close to baseline and the day-7 value is not. The higher EdU labeling after day-1 injury (5.2% against 0.8%) fits renewed cell-cycle activity, but without lineage tracing and a function measurement it does not show that new cardiomyocytes formed the missing tissue.

## Limits of this lesson

The numbers are synthetic and the model-organism findings are summarized, not reproduced. The lesson does not claim that any cell therapy, drug or procedure regenerates tissue in people, and it gives no clinical advice.
