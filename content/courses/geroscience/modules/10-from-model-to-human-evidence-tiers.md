# From model to human: evidence tiers, surrogate markers and honest limits

Most of what is known about slowing aging in the laboratory comes from yeast, worms, flies and mice. The question many people care about is whether any of it applies to humans, and how anyone would know. This lesson gives a way to place a claim on a ladder of evidence, to judge a biomarker offered as a stand-in for a health outcome, and to do the arithmetic that separates a large-sounding effect from a large effect. It also states plainly what this course does not claim.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Convert event rates to absolute and relative risk reduction and the number needed to treat, and interpret each.
2. Place a claim on an evidence ladder from cell culture to randomized human trial and name what is missing at each step.
3. Evaluate whether a biomarker change is an acceptable surrogate for a clinical outcome.

## The evidence ladder

Evidence about an intervention gets stronger and harder to obtain as it moves toward the target population.

1. **Cell culture and biochemistry** show that something can happen in a dish, with no guarantee about a whole organism.
2. **Invertebrate models** are fast and genetically tractable; they test conserved mechanisms but are far from human physiology.
3. **Rodent models** add organs, an immune system and a mammalian lifespan, and are the workhorse of lifespan studies. Results can depend on strain, sex, diet and housing.
4. **Nonhuman primates** are closer in physiology and live longer, which makes studies slow, small and expensive.
5. **Human observational studies** show associations in people but cannot separate the intervention from the kind of person who chooses it.
6. **Randomized human trials with clinical outcomes** can show benefit and harm, and are the standard for a treatment claim.

A claim should be placed at the highest rung for which direct evidence exists. "A drug extends mouse lifespan" sits at rung three; stating that it "slows aging" in people skips rungs four through six.

## Aging as a target

Aging as such is not an accepted medical indication, so human trials usually test a drug against a specific disease or functional decline. The **geroscience hypothesis** proposes that targeting basic aging biology could delay several age-related diseases together. It is a hypothesis; the reviews that set it out present it as a rationale for research, and whether any intervention achieves it in humans has to be shown by human trials with clinical endpoints. This course does not assert that any intervention slows or reverses human aging, and none of its lessons is medical advice. Products sold as anti-aging that lack such trials should be treated as unproven, and current trial registries and independent reviews are the place to check what has been tested.

## Biomarkers and surrogates

A **biomarker** is a measured quantity such as a blood level or an epigenetic clock estimate. A **surrogate endpoint** is a biomarker used in place of a clinical outcome (such as disease or death) because it is faster or cheaper to measure. A surrogate is only as good as the evidence that changing it changes the outcome. That evidence has to come from trials in which an intervention moved the biomarker and the outcome moved in step, and ideally across different kinds of interventions. Several drugs have improved a biomarker without improving, and sometimes while worsening, the clinical outcome, because the biomarker was one correlate among many pathways. Aging clocks are correlated with age and with risks, but whether lowering a clock value through an intervention lowers risk is, for most clocks and interventions, not established.

## Absolute and relative effects

Reports often give a **relative risk reduction (RRR)**, which sounds large, without the **absolute risk reduction (ARR)**, which says how many people benefit.

- ARR = risk in control − risk in treated.
- RRR = ARR / risk in control.
- **Number needed to treat (NNT)** = 1 / ARR: the number of people treated for one to avoid the event over the trial period, on average.

A twenty percent relative reduction of a rare event is a small absolute benefit; the same relative reduction of a common event is larger. Harms need the same treatment: the number needed to harm has the same form.

## Synthetic example

A hypothetical trial reports that over three years the event rate was 10% in the control group and 8% in the treated group, with a biomarker "age score" lower by 2 units in the treated group. The report headline reads "treatment cuts events by 20% and reverses biological age."

## Common mistakes

- Placing a claim higher on the evidence ladder than its evidence supports, as when extending mouse lifespan becomes slows aging.
- Reporting a relative risk reduction without the absolute risk reduction or the number needed to treat.
- Treating a biomarker change as a surrogate without trials linking its change to the outcome.
- Presenting the geroscience hypothesis as established.
- Reading marketing claims for an unproven product as evidence.
- Computing the number needed to treat as 1 divided by the relative risk reduction.

## Worked example

ARR = 0.10 − 0.08 = 0.02, or 2 percentage points. RRR = 0.02 / 0.10 = 20%. NNT = 1 / 0.02 = 50: on average 50 people are treated for three years for one to avoid the event. The headline's "reverses biological age" refers to the biomarker; without evidence that the score is a surrogate for the outcome in this setting, it supports a statement about the score, not about aging.

## Limits of this lesson

The trial is synthetic. This lesson does not recommend any treatment, supplement or test, and is not a substitute for clinical advice. Conclusions about real interventions require reading the actual trials and independent reviews.
