# Structure, electron flow and acid–base balance

## Learning objectives

1. Check valence-electron, charge and unsaturation accounting before interpreting a structure.
2. Distinguish resonance redistribution from an actual reaction using specified electron-pair movements.
3. Calculate a stipulated proton-transfer equilibrium and qualify reactivity conclusions.

## A formula is a constraint

A molecular formula constrains the possible structures but rarely identifies one. Count valence electrons using the atom inventory and adjust for net charge. For a neutral constructed formula C₅H₉NO, carbon supplies 20, hydrogen 9, nitrogen 5 and oxygen 6 electrons, totaling 40. Adding a negative charge would add one electron to the budget; it would not add an atom or establish where charge is localized.

For ordinary closed-shell formulas containing carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and halogens, the degree of unsaturation is (2C+2+N−H−X)/2. Oxygen does not enter this hydrogen-deficiency expression. The constructed C₅H₉NO formula gives two degrees of unsaturation. A ring or double bond contributes one, while a triple bond contributes two. Two degrees could therefore represent different combinations; the number alone does not prove a carbonyl, ring or particular constitutional isomer.

The counting rule has a domain. Unusual valences, radicals, charges and some elements require more careful treatment. A negative or half-integer result should prompt an inventory and assumption check rather than immediate assignment of a fractional ring. Formula arithmetic is an early filter for a structural proposal, not a replacement for bonding or analytical evidence.

## Formal charge and pair movement

Formal charge subtracts nonbonding electrons and half the bonding electrons from the atom's usual valence count. In a conventional singly bonded carboxylate oxygen, six valence electrons minus six nonbonding electrons minus one bond contribution gives −1. The carbonyl oxygen in the alternative contributor is neutral. Sum all formal charges and check that the total matches the species charge before comparing stability or reaction roles.

The [OpenStax resonance-rules section](https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/2-5-rules-for-resonance-forms) is a link-only conceptual reference. Original examples here use explicit pair bookkeeping. To exchange which oxygen bears the carboxylate double bond, move a lone pair from the singly bonded negative oxygen into its C–O bond, while moving the existing C=O π pair onto the other oxygen. Both pair movements are needed to avoid giving carbon five bonds.

The atoms and sigma connectivity stay fixed during that contributor comparison. Resonance drawings describe one delocalized species, rather than two isolable molecules rapidly converting into each other. Moving a proton would change which atoms are connected and belongs to an actual reaction or tautomeric comparison. A double-headed resonance connector and an equilibrium reaction arrow therefore encode different statements.

## Arrow tails carry information

An electron-pair arrow begins at a lone pair or bond and ends at the atom or bond receiving the pair. It does not begin at a positive charge just because that position is reactive. For a nucleophilic carbonyl addition, a donor lone pair forms a bond to carbonyl carbon while the carbonyl π pair moves to oxygen. The carbon avoids an expanded octet and the oxygen gains the corresponding electron density.

That description establishes a permissible electron-accounting step under the selected mechanism. It does not prove that any arbitrary donor reacts at an observable rate. Steric access, protonation, solvent and competing pathways matter. A formal charge or electronegativity comparison can suggest a reactive site while still falling short of a complete kinetic prediction.

## Proton transfer as an equilibrium model

For HA+B⇌A⁻+BH⁺ under matching activity conventions, K=10^(pK_a(BH⁺)−pK_a(HA)). Stipulate synthetic pK_a values 5 and 9 in one declared solvent and temperature convention. The equilibrium constant is 10⁴, favoring products in that reference comparison. These values identify no real acid or base and provide no reagent recommendation.

At imposed pH 6 for the synthetic HA with pK_a 5, the ideal deprotonated fraction is 10/(1+10)=10/11≈0.909091. At a different imposed pH the fraction changes, but analytical amount and charge still need compatible balances in a closed solution. An imposed-pH fraction calculation does not itself describe how to obtain that pH or establish every species present.

Basicity and nucleophilicity ask different questions. Basicity concerns a proton-transfer equilibrium, while nucleophilicity concerns a reaction rate toward an electrophile under specified conditions. Steric hindrance and solvation can change the relationship. A resonance-stabilized conjugate base can be less basic than a localized one without a universal ranking of every nucleophilic reaction following automatically.

## Worked example

Start from C₅H₉NO and independently count 40 valence electrons and two unsaturation units. Do not select a unique structure yet. For a carboxylate contributor exchange, identify the two simultaneous electron-pair movements and verify unchanged atom positions, net charge and carbon valence. For the separate synthetic acid/base pair, subtract the pK_a values in the correct direction to obtain K=10⁴. At imposed pH 6, normalize acid and base ratio 1:10 for the deprotonated fraction 10/11.

## Common mistakes

Do not move atoms while claiming only resonance. Do not draw a lone pair into an already full carbon valence without moving the outgoing π pair. Do not infer a unique functional group from unsaturation alone or equate basicity with every nucleophilic rate. The formula, charge, electron budget and arrow sequence should be checked together.

## Limits of this lesson
