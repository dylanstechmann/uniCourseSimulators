# Enolates, aldol connectivity and atom balance

## Learning objectives

1. Track proton removal and resonance-compatible enolate electron-pair movements.
2. Map donor and acceptor atoms into an aldol addition product and distinguish dehydration.
3. Check formula, stereochemical and selectivity claims against the stated reaction model.

## The alpha position has a specific meaning

The alpha carbon is adjacent to a carbonyl carbon. Removing an alpha hydrogen can produce an enolate whose electron distribution is represented by carbon-centered and oxygen-centered contributors. Ethanal, CH₃CHO, has three hydrogens on its alpha methyl carbon. The hydrogen attached directly to its carbonyl carbon is not one of those alpha hydrogens. Counting every molecular hydrogen as interchangeable ignores the bonding environment.

In a selected base-removal step, a base pair approaches the alpha hydrogen and the C–H bond pair is retained in the developing enolate system. A carbon-centered contributor has a lone pair on alpha carbon and the original C=O bond. A second contributor moves that pair into the adjacent C–C bond while the carbonyl π pair moves to oxygen. Atom positions and sigma connectivity are retained during this contributor comparison.

Proton movement between a carbonyl form and an enol is an actual tautomeric reaction, whereas electron redistribution between enolate contributors is a resonance description. The two should not be connected with the same explanation. An enol has an O–H bond that the corresponding enolate lacks under the stated proton inventory. Charge and atom counts distinguish these species even if their drawings share a C=C bond.

## Acid/base equilibrium first

For a synthetic carbonyl acid family with pK_a=20 and a base whose conjugate acid has pK_a=22 under the same convention, the proton-transfer reference constant is 10². The difference is supplied as a teaching parameter, not a tabulated value for a real base or an instruction to select one. The amount of enolate in a closed system still depends on starting inventories, activities and any subsequent consumption.

A stronger base can change accessible deprotonation, but solvent, aggregation and competing proton donors can matter. When two different alpha sites exist, the more rapidly formed enolate and the more stable equilibrium enolate need not be the same. A label such as kinetic or thermodynamic must name the comparison and assumptions rather than serve as an unqualified reagent rule.

## Make the carbon–carbon bond explicit

In an aldol addition, the donor enolate's alpha carbon forms a bond to the acceptor carbonyl carbon, while the acceptor C=O π pair goes to its oxygen. Subsequent proton transfer supplies the alcohol group. The donor carbonyl remains part of the product. The [OpenStax aldol section](https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/23-1-carbonyl-condensations-the-aldol-reaction) is a link-only reference for this selected connectivity distinction; no reaction conditions or teaching asset are imported.

For a paper self-addition of two ethanal units, label the donor carbonyl C1 and donor alpha carbon C2, then acceptor carbonyl C3 and acceptor methyl C4. The product chain is CHO(C1)–CH₂(C2)–CH(OH)(C3)–CH₃(C4). The new bond connects C2 to C3. The hydroxyl-bearing carbon is beta to the retained donor carbonyl, making a beta-hydroxy aldehyde rather than an alpha-hydroxy one.

Two C₂H₄O units sum to C₄H₈O₂ in the addition product. Addition itself loses no water in that net atom balance. If a later dehydration removes one water equivalent, the remaining formula is C₄H₆O, with an additional carbon–carbon double bond. Calling the addition step a condensation without distinguishing dehydration can hide where atoms went and which transformation was actually proposed.

## Product properties and stereochemistry

The simple addition product has one tetrahedral stereocenter at C3: its groups are H,OH,CH₃ and CH₂CHO. Under ideal achiral equal-face conditions, a pair of enantiomeric products can form. Exact equality is a model assumption rather than a measured outcome. More substituted donors and acceptors can create additional centers and diastereomeric possibilities requiring a fuller spatial analysis.

The addition product has one degree of unsaturation, supplied by the retained carbonyl. The dehydrated formula C₄H₆O has two, consistent with a carbonyl plus a new alkene in the stated chain. This check does not uniquely identify the alkene geometry. E/Z assignment needs actual substituents and spatial information, and it should not be inferred from formula alone.

## Crossed systems and competing routes

Mixing two enolizable carbonyl compounds can permit several donor/acceptor combinations. A drawing of one desired crossed product does not establish that it is selectively formed. Self-reactions, different alpha positions and additional additions can compete. A non-enolizable acceptor can remove some donor alternatives, but other reactivity and selectivity questions remain.

Reaction reversibility can also affect product distributions. A favorable carbon–carbon bond-forming step is not proof of complete conversion, and dehydration may change the network by removing or transforming an intermediate. A practical selectivity claim needs evidence about the actual products and amount balance. Here the atom mapping is a paper mechanism exercise with no reagent quantities, temperature program or synthesis procedure.

Atom labels provide a useful way to test a proposed mechanism. If a product hydroxyl oxygen is assigned to the donor rather than the acceptor in the selected addition, revisit the electron flow. If the carbon inventory changes before dehydration, identify an omitted species or an incorrect connection. These checks are more diagnostic than simply matching the word “aldol” to any structure containing an alcohol.

## Worked example

Identify ethanal's three alpha hydrogens and distinguish its aldehydic hydrogen. Apply the synthetic pK_a difference for reference proton transfer K=100. Label donor C1/C2 and acceptor C3/C4, then connect C2–C3 and retain donor C=O while converting acceptor C=O into the alcohol after proton transfer. Sum formulas for C₄H₈O₂, then subtract H₂O only for the separate dehydration to obtain C₄H₆O. Check the stereocenter and unsaturation count independently.

## Common mistakes

Do not confuse resonance with proton relocation, or attack through a donor atom without mapping the resulting connectivity. Do not lose water during an addition step whose stated atom balance retains it. Do not infer unique crossed selectivity or E/Z geometry from a name or formula.

## Limits of this lesson

The acid/base parameters and product-distribution assumptions are synthetic. The selected ethanal mapping illustrates elementary connectivity rather than a complete practical reaction. No synthesis or handling procedure is supplied. Original instruction has substantial AI assistance. The package remains partial, unreviewed and formative-only.
