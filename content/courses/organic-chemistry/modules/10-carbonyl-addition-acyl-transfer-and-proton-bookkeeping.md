# Carbonyl addition, acyl transfer and proton bookkeeping

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish carbonyl addition from addition–elimination using valid electron-pair and charge accounting.
2. Calculate a stipulated reversible adduct distribution and hydrolysis trajectory.
3. Explain why proton transfer, leaving-group ability and activation matter in the preserved amide case.

## The electrophilic carbonyl site

The C=O bond is polarized, making its carbon a possible electrophilic site. In a selected nucleophilic addition, the donor pair forms a C–Nu bond while the carbonyl π pair moves to oxygen. The central carbon then has four sigma bonds in a tetrahedral arrangement rather than five bonds. Its oxygen charge and any donor charge must be recorded instead of silently returning every atom to neutrality.

For a negatively charged nucleophile Nu⁻ adding to a neutral aldehyde or ketone, the selected alkoxide intermediate retains net charge −1. Oxygen has the negative formal charge after the π pair is moved to it. If a neutral amine such as NH₃ attacks instead, its nitrogen can become positively charged while oxygen is negative. That zwitterionic accounting differs from the anionic-donor example even though both form a tetrahedral carbon.

These are electron-accounting representations of stipulated steps. An arrow is not a measured reaction rate, and electrophilicity alone does not imply that every donor readily attacks. Steric environment, resonance donation, protonation and solvent affect which pathways are plausible. The functional-group comparison should begin with the actual derivative and possible outgoing group.

## Addition versus addition–elimination

A simple aldehyde or ketone lacks an attached heteroatom leaving group appropriate for ordinary nucleophilic acyl substitution. Addition and subsequent proton transfers therefore do not automatically eject an alkyl group as a free carbanion. An acyl derivative R–C(=O)–Z can instead permit collapse of a tetrahedral intermediate: oxygen's pair restores C=O while the C–Z bond pair departs with Z.

The identity and protonation state of Z matter. A good leaving group in one reaction context is not a universal label independent of acid/base conditions. Proton transfer can alter the charge and leaving behavior, and a neutral nucleophile can require deprotonation to give a neutral product. A complete proposed mechanism must account for every transferred proton and regenerate any catalyst claimed to be catalytic.

Amide resonance donation makes an ordinary amide carbonyl less reactive toward many nucleophilic substitutions than a corresponding activated derivative. That qualitative distinction does not provide a numerical hydrolysis rate or prove that no amide can react. Conditions and activation are central to the biological and synthetic examples, while this package supplies no real reagent procedure.

## A reversible adduct model

For a synthetic carbonyl C plus donor N forming adduct D, stipulate K_c=[D]/([C][N])=10 L/mol. Hold free N externally fixed at 0.20 mol/L, and total carbonyl-family concentration C_T=[C]+[D]=0.10 mol/L. The ratio D/C is 2, so adduct fraction is 2/3. Adduct concentration is 0.0666667 mol/L and free carbonyl is 0.0333333 mol/L.

This calculation assumes one selected adduct and an independently fixed free donor. If only a closed donor total is supplied, binding depletes it and a donor balance is required. Additional protonated donors or multiple adducts would also change the equation. A favorable reference ratio does not establish complete conversion or an actual equilibrium constant for an identified molecule.

## A distinct hydrolysis time model

For a separate hypothetical acyl compound under a stipulated pseudo-first-order loss constant 0.030 min⁻¹, initial amount 0.100 mol in a fixed system gives remaining amount 0.100exp(−0.030t). After 30 min, approximately 0.040657 mol remains. If one product equivalent forms per consumed starting compound without other pathways, product amount is approximately 0.059343 mol.

This trajectory is a kinetic construction, separate from the reversible adduct model. It assumes an effectively constant environment and no reverse or competing loss. It does not follow simply from the name “ester,” “amide” or “hydrolysis.” Product identity and stoichiometry still need evidence in a real system, and observed disappearance alone cannot tell whether every lost molecule formed the desired product.

## The preserved amide case

The existing self-assessment case asks why a carboxylic acid and amine in neutral water do not constitute a sufficient amide-forming mechanism. Acid/base transfer can change both partners, and the unactivated acid does not provide a universally competent departing group for the assumed condensation. Writing acid+amine→amide+water records a proposed net equation, not a demonstrated microscopic pathway or practical conversion.

Activation can change the reaction's accessible pathway, but an activated intermediate also has competing hydrolysis or other reactions. A proposed activation strategy must be paired with evidence for product identity and a material balance. The checklist names categories for discussion; this reading does not turn it into a preparation protocol or provide amounts, temperatures or instructions for an actual synthesis.

A chromatographic signal alone may represent starting material, hydrolysis product, activated intermediate or another species. Orthogonal structural evidence can help discriminate them. The later spectroscopy lesson examines this evidential distinction, and the synthetic lab will show why even resolved peak areas need a justified response model before becoming an amount ratio.

## Worked example

Describe donor-to-carbon and carbonyl-pair-to-oxygen movements for addition, then distinguish collapse with a declared outgoing Z group from an unsupported alkyl expulsion. For the independent adduct model, set D/C=10×0.20=2 and normalize the total 0.10. For the separate time model, calculate remaining 0.100exp(−0.9) and product by subtraction. Finally examine whether a proposed amide mechanism supplies activation, valid proton bookkeeping and product evidence.

## Common mistakes

Do not exceed carbon valence, omit donor charge changes or treat every carbonyl as the same derivative. Do not substitute total donor for fixed free donor without checking depletion. A balanced net condensation equation is not proof of a workable mechanism, and disappearance is not a product-identity measurement.

## Limits of this lesson

Adduct constants, amounts and loss rates are synthetic. The examples assess selected accounting steps rather than practical reaction optimization or a complete mechanism drawing. No synthesis or handling procedure is supplied. Original instruction has substantial AI assistance. The course remains partial, unreviewed and formative-only.
