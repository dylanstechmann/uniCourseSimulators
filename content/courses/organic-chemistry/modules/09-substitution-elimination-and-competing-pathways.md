# Substitution, elimination and competing pathways

## Learning objectives

1. Track electron pairs and spatial requirements in selected substitution and elimination steps.
2. Calculate competing pathway rates and product branching under stated kinetic assumptions.
3. Evaluate mechanism compatibility without treating a substrate label as a universal rule.

## Begin with the reacting bonds

Substitution replaces a leaving group with a new bond at the selected carbon. In a concerted SN2 step, a nucleophile's electron pair approaches the carbon opposite the leaving group while the carbon–leaving-group bond pair departs with that group. Bond formation and cleavage belong to one step; a free carbocation is not inserted into this particular mechanism. The [OpenStax SN2 section](https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/11-2-the-sn2-reaction) provides a link-only conceptual reference.

Spatial inversion follows at a tetrahedral reacting stereocenter in that elementary picture. Product R/S still needs fresh priority assignment, as the previous lesson explained. A strong nucleophile does not overcome every geometric obstacle: a tertiary center is sterically unfavorable for the usual backside approach. These model distinctions are more informative than memorizing one reagent name without specifying substrate or conditions.

In an SN1 picture, ionization moves the leaving bond pair onto the leaving group, producing a carbocation intermediate before nucleophilic capture. A planar intermediate can permit attack on either face, but an actual product ratio can be affected by ion pairs and the surrounding environment. Treat ideal equal-face capture as a stated assumption, not a guarantee of exact racemization in every real ionization reaction.

## Elimination requires a coordinated arrangement

In the selected E2 mechanism, a base pair goes toward a beta hydrogen, the C–H bond pair forms the new C=C bond and the C–leaving-group pair goes to the leaving group. These three pair movements occur together. The alpha carbon bears the leaving group; the beta carbon supplies the hydrogen. Removing a hydrogen from an unrelated position does not form the intended adjacent alkene.

The usual favorable arrangement is anti-periplanar beta hydrogen and leaving group. For a cyclohexane chair following the conventional trans-diaxial E2 pathway, both must be axial and oppositely directed. A ring flip changes axial availability but preserves each substituent's up/down identity. An equatorial leaving group cannot be treated as reactive in that chair just because a beta hydrogen exists somewhere on the ring.

Regioselectivity depends on accessible beta positions, steric factors and reaction conditions. The more substituted alkene is not guaranteed to dominate every elimination. A restricted anti arrangement can exclude an otherwise familiar product, and a bulky base can alter competition. A product prediction needs the actual substrate and conformations rather than a universal slogan.

## Two rates from one substrate

Use a synthetic fixed-environment model with substrate S consumed through two irreversible routes. Substitution rate is r_sub=k_sub[S][Nu], with k_sub=0.020 L/(mol·s), and elimination rate is r_elim=k_elim[S][B], with k_elim=0.010 L/(mol·s). Stipulate nearly constant free Nu=0.10 and B=0.20 mol/L, and initial S=0.050 mol/L. Each initial route rate is 0.000100 mol/(L·s).

The effective first-order constants are a=0.0020 and b=0.0020 s⁻¹. Substrate obeys dS/dt=−(a+b)S, so its half-life is ln 2/0.004≈173.2868 s. The substitution fraction of converted substrate is a/(a+b)=0.5 under the constant parallel-route assumptions. Equal branching here follows from the stipulated effective rates, not a universal prediction for any primary or secondary substrate.

Each route forms a distinct product in this simplified network, so product sum equals substrate consumed. At complete model conversion each product reaches 0.025 mol/L. At finite time the product amounts are 0.5(S₀−S), not 0.5S₀ regardless of conversion. Product ratio, conversion and recovered yield are separate quantities and should not be reported interchangeably.

## What a rate law can establish

Doubling Nu while holding B and the remaining conditions fixed doubles a in this construction. It increases the substitution branching fraction and accelerates total disappearance. If the same physical species plays both roles, however, its perturbation can change both effective constants; the independent-change calculation would describe a different constraint. State what is held fixed before comparing pathways.

An empirical second-order dependence can be compatible with a concerted mechanism, but it does not uniquely prove every microscopic event. Likewise, a first-order observed decay can arise from a bimolecular process with a nearly constant excess reactant. Intermediate-sensitive evidence and stereochemical observations can add constraints. This lesson provides no laboratory procedure for obtaining them.

Solvent affects solvation, ionization and nucleophile availability. Protonation can suppress a free donor while changing acid/base pathways. Temperature can change rate competition without simply choosing “substitution” or “elimination” as a switch. The declared constants summarize one synthetic environment and cannot be transplanted to another substrate or solvent as measured values.

## Worked example

Identify the two SN2 pair movements and the three E2 pair movements, checking that leaving and forming bonds match adjacent atoms. For the synthetic parallel network, multiply each second-order constant by its stipulated free reactant concentration to get a=b=0.0020 s⁻¹. Multiply by S₀ for the initial rates, add a+b for substrate decay and normalize a by that sum for the substitution branch. Check product sum against consumed S before interpreting selectivity.

## Common mistakes

Do not add a carbocation to a concerted SN2 step. Do not confuse a spatial inversion with an automatic descriptor swap. Check beta positions and anti access before selecting an alkene. Do not report branching fraction as conversion or ignore which free reactants were held constant.
