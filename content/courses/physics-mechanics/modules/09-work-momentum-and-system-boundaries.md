# Work, momentum and system boundaries

## Learning objectives

1. Apply momentum and energy balances to a selected collision system.
2. Distinguish net impulse from a contact-force contribution when gravity acts.
3. Calculate work for a position-dependent force and qualify what averages establish.

## Conservation starts with a boundary

Momentum conservation applies to a defined system when its external impulse is negligible over the chosen interval. Internal interaction forces can transfer momentum between parts without changing their total. That statement does not make each part's momentum constant. Draw the system boundary first and distinguish internal interactions from forces crossing it.

For a synthetic one-dimensional collision, take m₁=0.20 kg with v₁=+3.0 m/s and m₂=0.30 kg with v₂=−1.0 m/s. Stipulate that they stick and external impulse is negligible during collision. Initial total momentum is 0.60−0.30=+0.30 kg·m/s. Shared final velocity is 0.30/(0.20+0.30)=+0.60 m/s. Adding speed magnitudes instead would erase the opposing initial directions.

Initial kinetic energy is 0.5×0.20×3²+0.5×0.30×1²=1.05 J. Final kinetic energy is 0.5×0.50×0.60²=0.09 J. The kinetic reduction is 0.96 J even though momentum is conserved. Internal deformation, thermal energy or other unmodeled internal stores can account for that reduction; this paper collision does not identify their individual amounts.

## A collision does not conserve every quantity

An elastic collision adds a kinetic-energy constraint, while a sticking collision does not. Total energy conservation is broader than conservation of the mechanical kinetic portion. A claimed increase or decrease in momentum also requires checking external impulse, mass transfer and the selected frame rather than treating every disagreement as an arithmetic error.

The center-of-mass velocity follows total momentum divided by total mass in this closed example. It remains fixed when the net external force is zero, even as the individual velocities change. A different inertial frame changes the kinetic-energy values and momenta consistently; a physical classification such as sticking does not depend on merely changing the coordinate origin.

## Impulse concerns the force sum

Impulse is the time integral of net force, equal to momentum change for the fixed-mass point model. Suppose a separate 2.0 kg mass moves downward at 5.0 m/s and reaches rest over 0.10 s. Choose up positive. Momentum change is +10.0 kg·m/s, so average net force is +100 N. A support force acting upward is not identical to that net force while gravity remains downward.

Gravity contributes −mgΔt=−1.962 N·s over the interval. Thus contact impulse must be +11.962 N·s and average upward contact force 119.62 N. Its difference from net force is weight 19.62 N. These are averages over the declared time interval, not a peak force or an injury threshold. The result applies to a synthetic point mass, with no biological load recommendation.

The preserved impact lesson contains brief-stop approximations. The new example makes the distinction explicit: omitting gravitational impulse can be justified only when it is small relative to the stopping impulse for the intended accuracy. The dimensionless ratio gΔt/|Δv| estimates its scale here. A longer stopping interval can make gravity more relevant even while the net average needed for the same momentum change decreases.

## Work uses displacement rather than time

For a synthetic horizontal force F(x)=4+2x N with x in metres, work from x=0 to x=3 is the integral of F dx, giving 4×3+3²=21 J. If it is the net force on a 2.0 kg particle initially at rest, the work–kinetic-energy relation gives v=√21≈4.582576 m/s at the endpoint. The force grows with position, so multiplying only its initial value by three metres understates work.

This work integral is distinct from a force-time impulse. Their units also differ: joules versus newton-seconds. A specified force-position curve does not by itself provide elapsed time without a dynamical model. A force-time average and a force-displacement average can differ because the trajectory weights the force differently. Using the same word average does not make the two calculations interchangeable.

For conservative forces one can represent energy changes through a potential under the needed conditions. A dissipative force can transfer mechanical energy to other forms, and a prescribed driving force can add energy. Choose whether a force contributes external work or an internal energy change based on the selected system; do not count the same transfer twice.

## What can be inferred from averages

Equal impulse can arise from different force histories. A narrow high pulse and a broader low pulse can have the same time integral while different maxima. Likewise, equal net work can arise from different spatial force profiles. Neither integral alone establishes local contact stress, deformation distribution or material failure.

The force diagram and observation also need compatible intervals. A sensor reporting one contact component may omit gravity or other supports, whereas momentum change reflects the net sum. A disagreement can reveal a missing force or a reporting bias. Calibrating one channel does not automatically validate the entire mechanical model.

## Common mistakes

Do not claim kinetic-energy conservation for every collision, or confuse one-body contact force with the net force. Keep impulse and work integrals, signs, units and weighting intervals distinct. Averages do not determine peaks or material response.
