# Reaction extents and solution accounting

## Learning objectives

1. Balance equations and use stoichiometric extent to identify a limiting reactant.
2. Calculate theoretical yield, excess material and a measured-yield comparison.
3. Account for dissolved ions, precipitation assumptions and remaining concentrations.

## Balance atoms and charge

A balanced equation conserves each element and net electrical charge. Change coefficients, not chemical subscripts, to balance a specified reaction. For N₂+3H₂→2NH₃, each reaction event uses one nitrogen molecule and three hydrogen molecules to form two ammonia molecules. The same coefficients apply to molar amounts. The equation does not specify a laboratory synthesis method, reaction rate or equilibrium conversion.

Stoichiometric extent ξ is a common progress variable. Amounts change as n_i=n_i,0+ν_iξ, with signed coefficient ν negative for a reactant and positive for a product. Every reactant amount must remain nonnegative. The maximum stoichiometric extent is the smallest initial-reactant amount divided by its positive consumption coefficient, assuming the proposed reaction goes to that limit and no other process intervenes.

For the synthetic initial amounts 0.8 mol N₂ and 1.8 mol H₂, their extent limits are 0.8 and 1.8/3=0.6 mol. Hydrogen limits, giving ξ=0.6 mol and theoretical ammonia amount 1.2 mol. Nitrogen remaining is 0.8−0.6=0.2 mol. Choosing the reactant with fewer grams or simply fewer moles can give the wrong limiting reagent because coefficients and molar masses differ.

## Yield and mass checks

Use the stipulated rounded molar masses N₂=28.0 g/mol, H₂=2.0 g/mol and NH₃=17.0 g/mol in this paper example. The theoretical product mass is 1.2×17.0=20.4 g. Consumed reactant masses are 0.6×28.0=16.8 g and 1.8×2.0=3.6 g, totaling the same 20.4 g. The unused nitrogen mass is 5.6 g. This check follows the specified simplified masses and balanced reaction.

If a synthetic reported product mass is 15.3 g, percent yield is 100×15.3/20.4=75%. A lower recovered mass could reflect incomplete conversion, other reactions or recovery loss; the number alone does not distinguish them. A reported yield above 100% does not prove extra matter was created. It can indicate impurities, retained solvent, incorrect composition or a calculation problem.

Stoichiometric theoretical yield is a model ceiling for the stated reactants and product. It is not a prediction of actual kinetics or equilibrium behavior. The later equilibrium lesson shows why reactants can remain even when the bookkeeping would allow more product. Keep “available according to coefficients” distinct from “actually converted in a particular process.”

## Ionic species and a hypothetical precipitate

Consider the constructed reaction M²⁺(aq)+X²⁻(aq)→MX(s). It conserves charge and forms one formula unit per pair of ions. Suppose a paper mixture combines 100 mL of 0.020 mol/L M²⁺ with 150 mL of 0.010 mol/L X²⁻. Initial amounts are 0.0020 and 0.0015 mol. Assume quantitative precipitation, additive solution volumes and no competing equilibria. X²⁻ limits, giving 0.0015 mol MX.

If the stipulated molar mass of this hypothetical solid is 200.0 g/mol, its theoretical mass is 0.300 g. Excess M²⁺ amount is 0.0005 mol in final volume 0.250 L, giving 0.0020 mol/L or 2.0 mM. The remaining concentration uses the final mixed volume, not the original 100 mL alone. The symbols M and X are invented species for accounting, not a recommendation to combine actual reagents.

The spectator counterions needed for overall solution electroneutrality are omitted from the net ionic equation, but they have not disappeared from the mixture. A full ionic equation would include them on both sides before cancellation. A solid formation model also needs a solubility or equilibrium justification before being applied to a real system; “quantitative precipitation” is an explicit assumption here, not a universal property of every pair of ions.

## Dilution is not reaction

In dilution without reaction or material loss, solute amount is conserved and C₁V₁=C₂V₂. In a reacting mixture, use mole accounting first and then divide surviving amounts by final volume. Applying the dilution equation to a species consumed by reaction ignores its changing amount. Conversely, a concentration decrease by volume expansion does not prove chemical consumption.

For the hypothetical precipitation example, M²⁺ would have concentration 8 mM after simple dilution of its initial 0.002 mol into 0.250 L if no reaction occurred. The stated reaction leaves 2 mM instead. Both calculations use the same final volume, but different conserved or consumed amounts. That comparison makes the reaction assumption visible.

## Worked example

For N₂+3H₂→2NH₃, compare extent limits 0.8 and 0.6 mol, choose 0.6 and obtain 1.2 mol product plus 0.2 mol unused nitrogen. Convert product using the supplied 17.0 g/mol to get 20.4 g, then compare synthetic recovery 15.3 g for 75% yield. For the separate ionic example, compare 0.0020 and 0.0015 mol, form 0.0015 mol solid and divide excess 0.0005 mol by the combined 0.250 L to obtain 2 mM. No physical experiment is implied by either calculation.

## Common mistakes and checks

Do not alter subscripts to balance an equation. Do not identify a limiting reactant without dividing by coefficients. Do not use initial volume for a final-mixture concentration. Include all required spectator species when checking full charge balance. Separate theoretical yield, actual conversion and recovered product purity.

## Limits of this lesson

Amounts, yields and hypothetical precipitate properties are synthetic. Completion and additive-volume assumptions simplify the examples. No chemical handling or synthesis protocol is supplied. Practice checks selected amounts and interpretations rather than a complete experimental mass balance. Original instruction has substantial AI assistance; the package remains partial, unreviewed and formative-only.
