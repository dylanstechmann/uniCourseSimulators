# Chromosome segregation errors: nondisjunction, aneuploidy and maternal age

Every egg and sperm should carry exactly one copy of each chromosome. When chromosomes fail to separate during meiosis, a process called nondisjunction, gametes with an extra or missing chromosome result, and a zygote formed from one of them is **aneuploid**: trisomic (three copies) or monosomic (one copy). Aneuploidy occurs in at least 5% of clinically recognized human pregnancies and is the leading genetic cause of pregnancy loss (Hassold and Hunt, 2001). Its frequency also rises with the mother's age, which makes egg aneuploidy one of the clearest examples of a cellular process that declines with age. This lesson traces how errors arise, computes their frequencies with synthetic numbers and separates a descriptive age pattern from its proposed mechanisms.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Trace the gametes produced by nondisjunction in meiosis I versus meiosis II and the resulting zygotes (normal, trisomic or monosomic).
2. Calculate expected aneuploid gamete frequencies from per-meiosis error rates, and compare age groups with a relative risk and a risk difference.
3. Evaluate evidence about why segregation errors increase with maternal age, distinguishing a description from a mechanism.

## Two places to go wrong

Meiosis has two divisions. In **meiosis I**, homologous chromosomes (one from each parent) separate. In **meiosis II**, sister chromatids separate.

- **Nondisjunction in meiosis I:** both homologs go to the same pole. Of the four products, two carry both homologs (disomic, n + 1) and two carry none of that chromosome (nullisomic, n − 1). None is normal.
- **Nondisjunction in meiosis II** (in one of the two secondary cells): that cell gives one disomic and one nullisomic product, while the other secondary cell divides normally. Of the four products, two are normal, one is disomic and one is nullisomic.

Fertilization by a normal gamete turns a disomic gamete into a **trisomic** zygote and a nullisomic one into a **monosomic** zygote. Most autosomal monosomies and trisomies are lost early in development; trisomy 21 is one of the few autosomal trisomies compatible with survival to birth, although many trisomy 21 pregnancies are also lost.

Molecular markers can tell the two errors apart, using markers at which the parent is heterozygous. Near the centromere, an extra chromosome from a meiosis I error carries the two different homologs of the parent (heterodisomy), while one from a meiosis II error carries two copies of the same homolog (isodisomy). Recombination between the centromere and the marker can blur this, so markers close to the centromere are used.

## Frequencies from error rates

**Synthetic rate:** suppose that in a fraction e = 0.04 of meioses one particular chromosome pair undergoes nondisjunction. Count all four products of each meiosis, as in sperm production where every product matures. If every error is in meiosis I, the fraction of products that are disomic for that chromosome is e × 2/4 = 0.02. If every error is in meiosis II of one secondary cell, it is e × 1/4 = 0.01.

Eggs need a separate argument, because in oogenesis only one of the four products becomes the egg and the others are polar bodies. After a meiosis I error no product is normal, so whichever one becomes the egg is abnormal (disomic or nullisomic with equal chance). After a meiosis II error the egg is abnormal only if the error happened in the division that produces the egg and not in the polar body's division. Per-product and per-egg fractions therefore differ for meiosis II errors, and a calculation should say which one it reports.

## Describing the age pattern

**Synthetic registry:** among 4000 recognized pregnancies in a younger maternal-age band there are 6 with trisomy 21, and among 1000 in an older band there are 12.

- Risk in the younger band: 6/4000 = 0.0015 (1.5 per 1000).
- Risk in the older band: 12/1000 = 0.012 (12 per 1000).
- **Relative risk** = 0.012/0.0015 = 8.0; **risk difference** = 10.5 per 1000 pregnancies.

Both summaries matter. The relative risk shows how strongly risk depends on age; the risk difference shows how many additional affected pregnancies that means per 1000. A relative risk of 8 sounds dramatic, but most pregnancies in either band are unaffected. With synthetic counts this small, both numbers are also uncertain; a confidence interval would be wide.

## From pattern to mechanism

An age curve describes risk; it does not explain it. Human oocytes enter meiosis I before birth and then pause for decades until ovulation, so a 40-year-old's egg has been held in meiosis I for about 40 years. Leading explanations for the age effect include gradual loss of the cohesin protein complexes that hold chromosomes together during that long arrest, and differences in where crossovers formed in the fetal ovary, since crossovers help homologs stay paired. Each is a hypothesis that predicts specific observations, such as more meiosis I errors, weaker sister-chromatid cohesion in older oocytes, or particular crossover patterns in trisomic offspring. Testing such predictions requires direct measurements in oocytes and model organisms, not more age curves. Paternal age has a much smaller effect on whole-chromosome aneuploidy than maternal age.

## Common mistakes

- Confusing meiosis I errors (no normal products) with meiosis II errors (half of the products normal).
- Assuming every disomic gamete gives a live birth with trisomy; most aneuploid conceptuses are lost.
- Reporting only a relative risk without the absolute risk or risk difference.
- Treating an association with age as evidence for a particular mechanism.
- Forgetting that recombination can make a meiosis I error look like a meiosis II error at distal markers.

## Worked example

**Problem.** In a synthetic set of 50 trisomic conceptions, genotyping a marker close to the centromere shows two different maternal alleles in 38 and two identical maternal alleles in 12. What does this suggest, and what fraction of errors occurred in maternal meiosis I?

**Step 1: classify.** At markers where the mother is heterozygous, two different maternal alleles near the centromere indicate a meiosis I error (heterodisomy); two copies of one maternal allele indicate a meiosis II error (isodisomy).

**Step 2: estimate.** 38/50 = 0.76 of the errors appear to be meiosis I errors.

**Step 3: qualify.** Recombination near the marker or a pre-meiotic error could misclassify a few cases, and with 50 cases the 0.76 has a confidence interval of roughly ±0.12. The pattern is consistent with most maternal errors arising in meiosis I.
