# Oxygen delivery: arterial content, the Fick principle and extraction

Every tissue's oxygen supply is the product of how much oxygen each liter of blood carries and how many liters reach it. That product, together with how much the tissue removes, explains why anemia, low cardiac output and lung disease each threaten tissues in different ways, and why the body has several ways to compensate. The same mass balance underlies how cardiac output was first measured. This lesson computes oxygen content, cardiac output by the Fick principle, delivery and extraction from synthetic values.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Calculate arterial and venous oxygen content from hemoglobin, saturation and partial pressure.
2. Apply the Fick principle to compute cardiac output, oxygen delivery and the extraction ratio.
3. Predict how anemia and changes in cardiac output alter delivery and extraction, and which compensations are available.

## Oxygen content

Blood carries oxygen in two forms. Each gram of fully saturated hemoglobin binds about 1.34 mL of oxygen, and plasma dissolves about 0.003 mL per deciliter per mmHg of partial pressure. So

**C_O₂ = 1.34 × Hb × S + 0.003 × P_O₂** (mL O₂ per dL blood),

with Hb in g/dL and S as a fraction. With Hb = 15 g/dL, S_a = 0.98, and P_a = 100 mmHg, arterial content is 19.70 + 0.3 = 20.0 mL/dL. Dissolved oxygen contributes only 1.5% of the total: content depends on hemoglobin and saturation far more than on partial pressure once hemoglobin is nearly saturated. That is the reason breathing extra oxygen raises the content of normal arterial blood very little.

## The Fick principle

Mass balance across the lungs or across the whole body at steady state says that oxygen uptake equals blood flow times the arteriovenous content difference:

**V̇O₂ = Q̇ × (C_a − C_v).**

Measure any three and the fourth follows. With mixed-venous S_v = 0.75 and P_v = 40 mmHg, C_v = 15.20 mL/dL and the difference is 4.80 mL/dL (48.0 mL per liter). If whole-body uptake is V̇O₂ = 250 mL/min,

Q̇ = 250 / 48.0 = 5.21 L/min.

## Delivery and extraction

Oxygen delivery is **DO₂ = Q̇ × C_a** = 5.21 L/min × 200 mL/L = 1041 mL/min. The **extraction ratio** is V̇O₂ / DO₂ = 0.24: at rest the body uses about a quarter of what is delivered, leaving a reserve. Extraction varies widely between organs; the heart extracts a large fraction even at rest, so it depends on raising flow when demand rises, while the kidney extracts little relative to its large flow.

## Perturbations and compensation

**Anemia.** At Hb = 10 g/dL with the same saturation, C_a falls to 13.4 mL/dL. If cardiac output and uptake were unchanged, extraction would have to rise to 0.36. Alternatively, keeping extraction at 0.24 would require cardiac output of 7.7 L/min. The body uses both: cardiac output rises, and tissues extract more, which lowers venous saturation.

**Low cardiac output.** Delivery falls in proportion to flow; tissues compensate by raising extraction until venous oxygen falls too low to drive diffusion into cells. A falling mixed-venous saturation is a sign that delivery is not keeping up with demand.

**Low saturation** (for example, at altitude or with lung disease) lowers content directly. Over days, the kidney's erythropoietin response raises hemoglobin.

During exercise, uptake can rise more than tenfold in trained people, through both higher cardiac output and higher extraction by working muscle. The maximum, V̇O₂max, declines with age on average, and the Fick equation shows the two places such a decline can come from: maximal flow and maximal extraction.

## Worked example

**Problem.** During synthetic hard exercise, whole-body uptake rises to 1500 mL/min. Arterial content stays at 20.0 mL/dL, and mixed-venous saturation falls to 0.4 with P_v = 25 mmHg. Estimate cardiac output and extraction.

**Step 1: venous content.** C_v = 1.34 × 15 × 0.4 + 0.003 × 25 = 8.12 mL/dL.

**Step 2: Fick.** The arteriovenous difference is 11.88 mL/dL = 118.8 mL/L, so Q̇ = 1500 / 118.8 = 12.6 L/min.

**Step 3: extraction.** V̇O₂ / (Q̇ × C_a) = 1500 / (12.6 × 200) = 0.59. Uptake rose 6-fold, delivered by a 2.4-fold rise in flow and a 2.5-fold rise in extraction. Both factors have upper limits, which together set the maximum uptake.

**Step 4: how this is measured.** The direct Fick method needs a measured oxygen uptake (from expired gas), an arterial sample and a true mixed-venous sample from the pulmonary artery, because venous blood from different organs differs in content. That invasiveness is why indirect methods are common, and why each method's assumptions are reported with its numbers. The calculation itself is only as good as the steady state it assumes: during a rapid change in workload, uptake measured at the mouth lags what the muscles are using.

## Limits of this lesson

All values are synthetic and round. The constant 1.34 mL/g is a commonly used approximation (values from 1.31 to 1.39 appear), carbon monoxide and abnormal hemoglobins are ignored, and the whole-body Fick estimate assumes a steady state and well-mixed venous blood.
