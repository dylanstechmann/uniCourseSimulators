# Cytoskeletal systems, adhesion, and extracellular matrix

Cell shape and tissue organization emerge from connected molecular systems. Cytoskeletal filaments organize the interior of a cell, adhesion complexes connect cells to neighboring cells or extracellular matrix (ECM), and the matrix itself carries both biochemical and mechanical information. These structures change over time; they are not passive building materials.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

1. Compare the major structural and transport roles of actin filaments, microtubules, and intermediate filaments while accounting for their dynamic assembly.
2. Trace a cell–matrix adhesion route from an ECM ligand through an integrin and cytoskeletal linker, and contrast it with selected cell–cell junctions.
3. Relate ECM composition and remodeling to a testable cellular measurement without treating one matrix ingredient as a complete tissue environment.

## Three filament systems with distinct jobs

The term **cytoskeleton** describes a dynamic network of filaments and associated proteins. Three major filament systems are common in animal cells, but their properties and tasks differ.

| System | Building blocks and organization | Common roles | Example motor or associated proteins |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Actin filaments | Actin subunits assemble into polarized filaments that can reorganize rapidly. | Cell cortex, membrane protrusion, contractile structures, cell crawling, and parts of cell division. | Myosins generate or transmit force along actin; cross-linkers organize bundles and networks. |
| Microtubules | α/β-tubulin subunits form polarized hollow polymers whose ends can grow or shrink. | Spatial organization of organelles, long-range intracellular transport, cilia/flagella, and chromosome movement during division. | Kinesins and dynein move cargo in context-dependent directions along microtubules. |
| Intermediate filaments | Tissue-dependent proteins assemble into rope-like networks with different mechanical behavior from actin or tubulin polymers. | Resilience to deformation and tensile stress; support of cell shape and specialized structures. | Keratins, vimentin, neurofilaments, and desmin are examples of tissue-associated families. |

Actin and tubulin subunits bind and hydrolyze nucleotides, and their polymer ends can turn over while the filament remains useful. Intermediate filaments also remodel, though their assembly dynamics differ from those of actin and microtubules. The three systems interact through accessory proteins. A cell's mechanical integrity is therefore not explained by drawing three independent cables.

Motor proteins convert chemical energy into movement or force. A myosin can move along actin or link actin networks under some conditions; kinesin and dynein families transport cargo along microtubules. The direction, speed, load, and outcome depend on motor family, cargo, track polarity, adaptor proteins, and cell context. A motor's name does not reveal the fate of every cargo it binds.

## The extracellular matrix is an organized, remodeled environment

The **extracellular matrix** is a locally organized mixture outside cells. Collagens can provide structural networks; fibronectin and laminins can provide adhesive ligands and assembly sites; proteoglycans and associated molecules contribute hydration, spacing, and interactions with signaling factors. Composition varies by tissue and developmental or repair state. Cells can deposit, cross-link, bind, cleave, and reorganize matrix components; the matrix is not a fixed scaffold.

ECM composition, architecture, and mechanics are related but distinct variables. Two matrices with the same bulk stiffness can differ in ligand identity, ligand accessibility, fiber alignment, porosity, degradability, and local growth-factor retention. Conversely, changing a cross-linker to alter stiffness may also change one or more of those properties. A useful experiment measures the property named in its claim and matches or reports the plausible alternatives.

## Adhesion links outside to inside

**Integrins** are heterodimeric transmembrane receptors that bind many ECM ligands and connect to intracellular adaptor proteins. A simplified cell–matrix route is:

`collagen / fibronectin / laminin → integrin αβ → talin and other adaptors → actin-associated structures`

With actin and associated proteins, clustered integrins can form **focal adhesions** that transmit force and organize signaling. Integrins can respond to intracellular state as well as extracellular ligand and mechanical context. They are more than anchors, but their presence alone does not prove which signaling branch or cell response followed.

Other junctions make different connections. **Cadherins** can connect neighboring cells and link to cortical cytoskeletal networks through associated proteins. Epithelial **hemidesmosomes** use a specific integrin complex to connect cells to matrix through intermediate filaments; desmosomes also provide strong cell–cell connections to intermediate-filament networks. These are useful examples, not a full catalog of adhesion families.

Keeping the junction type explicit prevents several errors. A matrix-receptor perturbation does not automatically test cell–cell adhesion. A focal-adhesion image does not by itself show force magnitude. A change in cell spreading could reflect attachment, survival, spreading, cell-cycle state, or an altered matrix ligand rather than a unique mechanotransduction mechanism.

## Worked example: trace an adhesion claim

Suppose a perturbation changes cell spreading on a fibronectin-coated surface. The surface ligand makes an integrin-linked route plausible, but the image alone does not establish that integrin signaling caused the change. A more discriminating design measures attachment and viability, matches coating density and substrate chemistry, confirms the perturbation's target engagement, and measures the proposed downstream variable. An orthogonal perturbation or a specific rescue can strengthen attribution. The conclusion remains limited to the measured cells and coating conditions.

## Read a junction diagram as an experiment plan

When interpreting a drawing or assay, label each element as **measured**, **perturbed**, or **inferred**. Then ask:

- Which ECM ligand was present, and was its accessible density measured or matched?
- Was the experiment testing cell–matrix or cell–cell adhesion?
- Which filament system and adaptor were directly measured?
- Does the readout measure attachment, force, signaling, or a later outcome?
- Were viable-cell number and cell shape defined before analysis?
- Could altered matrix remodeling or ligand accessibility explain the result?

A junction schematic is a model for choosing controls; it is not the result of those controls.

## Limits of this lesson

This lesson introduces the three filament systems, common matrix components, integrin-linked adhesions, and examples of cell–cell junctions. It does not provide a complete family-by-family receptor survey, a tissue atlas of matrix composition, or a laboratory protocol for culturing cells on biomaterials. Mechanical measurement and mechanotransduction are developed separately in the next lesson.
