# Protein sequence, structure, and variant evidence

## Four structural levels, with different evidence

A protein is one or more polypeptide chains whose amino-acid sequence constrains the interactions that are possible. **Primary structure** is the residue sequence. Local backbone patterns such as alpha helices and beta sheets are **secondary structure**, stabilized largely by backbone hydrogen bonds. A single chain's three-dimensional arrangement is its **tertiary structure**. The arrangement of multiple chains in a complex is **quaternary structure**. These levels are a useful vocabulary; they are not four independent layers that can be inferred from a sequence list alone.

Side chains vary in size, charge, polarity, and flexibility. Nonpolar residues are often buried in soluble protein interiors, while polar and charged residues are often exposed to water. This is a tendency rather than a universal rule: membrane-spanning surfaces, ligand pockets, transient interfaces, and intrinsically disordered regions have different environments. Charged residues can form electrostatic interactions, polar groups can hydrogen-bond, and tightly packed surfaces make many weak contacts. Some proteins also use covalent disulfides, especially in appropriate oxidizing compartments.

The native state is a distribution of conformations. Temperature, pH, salts, binding partners, post-translational modifications, and the crowded cellular environment can shift that distribution. Chaperones assist some proteins in reaching productive states but do not encode a unique structure independently of the sequence. A structure model is evidence about plausible geometry; it is not a direct measurement of abundance, dynamics, activity, localization, or phenotype.

## Worked example: a buried hydrophobic residue becomes charged

Suppose a leucine in a soluble protein's buried core is changed to aspartate. Leucine is nonpolar; aspartate is usually negatively charged near neutral pH. The substitution could make the local environment less favorable and destabilize the folded population. That prediction does not mean the protein must be absent or inactive: the residue may be partly solvent-exposed, a nearby positive charge may compensate, the protein may adopt an alternate structure, or the assay may tolerate reduced stability.

Design measurements that separate these possibilities. First verify the variant sequence and compare wild-type and variant abundance using a matched loading control. Measure soluble and insoluble fractions to detect aggregation. A folding-sensitive method such as circular dichroism or a thermal-stability assay can test changes in the purified protein, but each method has its own assumptions. Measure the relevant activity at matched protein concentration; lower cellular activity could otherwise reflect lower abundance rather than altered specific activity. A revertant or compensatory mutation can provide a stronger mechanistic test. Replicate the measurements and report uncertainty. If the variant changes several readouts, explain which causal model is supported and which alternatives remain.

## Read variant evidence in layers

Sequence conservation can identify positions that have been constrained across sampled relatives, but conservation alone does not specify the mechanism. A predicted structure can suggest whether a residue is buried, near a ligand, or at an interface, but model confidence and cellular context matter. Biochemical assays test a measured property under defined conditions. Cell-based assays add localization, partners, and regulation but also introduce more possible causes. Patient or organism phenotypes are further downstream and require careful control of genetic background and environment.

Separate these claims: “the residue is conserved,” “the substitution changes thermal stability in this assay,” “the variant changes signaling in this cell type,” and “the variant causes a clinical phenotype.” Each requires distinct evidence. A sound explanation states the link between one measurement and the next claim instead of jumping from a sequence change to a whole-organism conclusion.

## Check your understanding

1. A charged substitution is predicted at a protein surface and has no measured effect on purified-protein stability. What does this result rule out, and what does it leave unresolved?
2. A variant has normal mRNA abundance, lower protein abundance, and normal enzyme activity when equal amounts of purified protein are tested. Which explanation is supported more strongly than a catalytic defect?
3. Why is a structure prediction insufficient to conclude that a variant causes a phenotype in a patient?
