# Covalent architecture, functional groups, and biological molecules

## Why a structure diagram is a model

Biological molecules are built from atoms joined by covalent bonds. A line-angle or ball-and-stick drawing records which atoms are connected and, sometimes, their approximate geometry. It does not show the constant motion of atoms, the surrounding water, or the distribution of molecular conformations in a cell. Use the drawing to state a chemical possibility; use measurement to decide whether that possibility matters in a particular biological setting.

Carbon has four valence electrons and can form four covalent bonds, which permits chains, branches, and rings. Oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorus add chemically distinct groups. The local arrangement matters: a carbonyl oxygen is a hydrogen-bond acceptor, whereas an alcohol hydroxyl can donate and accept a hydrogen bond; a protonated amine is positively charged, while an uncharged amine is not. The same atoms arranged in a different connectivity or stereochemistry can have different properties.

## Functional groups provide useful, limited predictions

Common biological groups include hydroxyls, carbonyls, carboxyls, amino groups, thiols, phosphates, and methyl groups. A phosphate group is often negatively charged near physiological pH and interacts strongly with water and counterions. A methyl group is nonpolar and can contribute to a hydrophobic surface. A carboxyl group can be protonated or deprotonated; its population depends on pH and local environment. These are tendencies, not labels that determine a molecule's complete behavior.

At the level of macromolecules, covalent linkages connect residues. Peptide bonds join amino acids, phosphodiester bonds join nucleotides, and glycosidic bonds join sugars. In each case, the polymer's sequence and connectivity constrain later interactions. For example, the peptide backbone contains a repeating carbonyl and amide pattern, while each amino-acid side chain contributes a different size, charge, and interaction surface. Two proteins can share many amino acids yet differ at a small number of positions that change local chemistry; whether those changes alter folding or function must be tested.

The phrase “water is removed” is a useful bookkeeping shorthand for some condensation reactions, but it is not a complete biochemical mechanism. Cells synthesize polymers through enzyme-catalyzed pathways that use activated substrates and energy coupling. Hydrolysis adds water across a bond in the net stoichiometry, yet the reaction rate and direction in a cell depend on enzymes, substrate and product concentrations, and coupling. Do not infer that an isolated covalent bond simply forms because two monomers are nearby.

## Worked example: a cysteine-to-serine substitution

Suppose a cysteine in a soluble protein is replaced by serine. Both side chains are similar in size and have a polar atom, but cysteine has a sulfur-containing thiol whereas serine has an oxygen-containing hydroxyl. Cysteine can form a disulfide bond if two thiols are brought together in an oxidizing environment; serine cannot make that same covalent crosslink. Both groups can participate in hydrogen-bonding interactions, but their geometry and polarizability differ.

The defensible prediction is conditional: if the cysteine participates in a disulfide that stabilizes the folded protein, the substitution may reduce that stabilization. The sequence change alone does not prove that the protein will be absent, misfolded, or inactive. First establish whether the residue is conserved and whether the protein is expected to encounter an oxidizing compartment. Then compare matched wild-type and variant constructs, measure abundance and solubility, and measure the relevant activity at matched protein concentration. Include an expression or loading control, an assay blank or background control where appropriate, and a rescue or revertant when feasible. Each measurement rules out a different alternative explanation.

## Reasoning checklist

For a structure-function claim, record (1) the exact chemical change, (2) the environment and protonation state, (3) the interaction or covalent linkage that could change, (4) the predicted measurable consequence, and (5) at least one competing explanation. Distinguish a chemical prediction from an experimentally observed phenotype. Do not treat “polar,” “charged,” or “hydrophobic” as complete functional mechanisms.

## Check your understanding

1. Which functional-group property changes when a carboxyl group becomes deprotonated? State the pH dependence you would expect.
2. A cysteine is conserved across several homologs, but the protein still folds after a Cys-to-Ser substitution in bacteria. Name two measurements that would distinguish loss of a disulfide from altered activity or localization in its native cell.
3. Explain why an amino-acid sequence constrains, but does not by itself report, a protein's full ensemble in a living cell.
